Section 01 — Introduction

What is higher
education in CS?

A look at the different paths, what they're actually for, and who they're designed to serve.

After a bachelor's degree in Computer Science, you broadly have three paths: join the workforce, pursue a taught master's (MS), or pursue a research degree (MS or PhD). These are very different things in terms of duration, learning outcomes and skill building.

Higher education is not a sequel to a bachelor's. It is a different kind of institution with very different incentives, that may not appeal to everyone. Your decision depends on whether you believe that higher education is indispensable for the career you aim to build.

The three paths at a glance

Path 01

Coursework MS

1.5–2 years. Structured classes. Designed to make you a better thinker rather than a better coder. Courses are usually specialisations in CS topics such as ML, systems, or HCI. Ends in a project or capstone under the department. Serves for entry into better industry roles.

Path 02

Research MS

1.5–2.5 years. Mix of courses and independent research. Ends in a thesis under an advisor. This serves as a trial run for a PhD. Useful if you want to test research before committing to 4–6 years.

Path 03

PhD

4–6 years. You are no longer a student in any traditional sense. You are a researcher who happens to take occasional courses while working in a lab full time. You produce new knowledge, are paid a stipend and your relationship with your advisor is central to your experience.

What higher education is not

It is not a safe option when you're unsure what to do. It is not a signal of intelligence — it is a signal of a specific kind of interest. It is not always the highest-paying path. And in CS, a master's is NOT required to do high-quality work in industry.

Many of the best software engineers, ML practitioners, and engineering leaders in India and abroad do not have postgraduate degrees. Some of the best researchers do.

The Indian CS context

If you're at Ashoka, you likely have peers going to IIT master's programmes, BITS dual-degree alumni entering the market, and some going abroad to universities like CMU, UMass, or UT Austin. Here's what's worth knowing:

India

IIT / IISc MS & MTech

Heavily research-oriented. Strong brand in Indian academia and some industry roles. Stipends for research MS. Competitive admissions via GATE / direct interview for top candidates.

Abroad

US / Europe MS

Coursework MS programmes (CMU, Columbia, UCSD) are self-funded or loan-based. Expensive ($60–90K total). Strong return if you intend to stay or work abroad. Return on investment is real but not guaranteed.

Both

PhD — fully funded

A PhD should never be self-funded. Both IIITs/IISc in India and most US/EU programmes fund PhD students with stipends. If someone asks you to pay for a PhD, that is a red flag.

What Ashoka prepares you for

Ashoka's CS programme is liberal-arts inflected — you've likely done courses outside CS, thought about ethics, policy, and systems in ways that most engineering students haven't. This is actually a meaningful differentiator for research (interdisciplinary problems) and for some MS programmes that value breadth.

It can also mean your coding interview prep may need work compared to peers from engineering colleges. Be honest with yourself about this gap before applying.

Section 02 — Reality Check

What does an MS
or PhD actually look like?

The lived reality of a student pursuing higher education. What do you spend your days doing, and what does that demand of you?

Students applying to graduate school should have a clear idea of their lifestyle for the next few years, especially how it differs from their undergraduate education.

A week in a coursework MS

Mon–Tue

Lectures and readings

Attend 2–3 classes. Read papers and textbooks assigned for the week. CS master's courses move fast — a week may cover material that took a month in undergrad.

Wed–Thu

Assignments and problem sets

Most courses have weekly homework. Expect to code, derive, and debug. In systems or ML courses, assignments can take 10–20 hours each. Group work is common but graded individually at most schools.

Fri–Sun

Projects and catching up

Term projects run alongside coursework. Most students spend weekends on these. Social life exists, but it is not the same as undergrad. You are working most of the time.

A week in a PhD programme

After the first year of coursework, a PhD student's week looks very different, and rather unstructured.

Variable

Reading papers

A serious PhD student reads 3–10 papers a week, depending on the stage of their research. This is how you understand what already exists and where the gaps are.

Variable

Running experiments

In CS, this usually means writing code, training models, running ablations, and debugging for days with no visible progress. A single paper can take 6–18 months of experimental work.

Weekly

Advisor meetings

Most PhD students meet their advisor once a week. This meeting is the compass of your programme. A good advisor gives direction and opens doors. A mismatched or absent advisor is one of the most common reasons PhDs stall or fail.

Ongoing

Writing

Papers, grant proposals, literature reviews. Strong academic writing is essential and very different from technical writing. Many students underestimate how much of a PhD is writing.

The advisor relationship

This is the most important factor in a PhD experience, and almost no one talks about it before they apply. Your advisor controls your funding, your publication record, your graduation timeline, and often your first job.

Ask them

Graduation timeline

How long do their students typically take? Are there students who left without finishing? Why?

Ask current students

Mentoring style

Hands-on or hands-off? Some students need direction; others need autonomy. Know which you are.

Red flags

Advisor warning signs

No recent publications from their students. No students willing to talk to you. High rate of students changing advisors or leaving the programme.

The core of a strong PhD application

A strong PhD application is anchored in a clearly defined and well-thought-out research question. Admissions committees are not just looking for interest in a field — they want to see that you have already begun to narrow down a specific problem you care about and can articulate why it matters. This question should feel purposeful and feasible, not vague or overly broad.

Just as importantly, you need to demonstrate that you have the skills to pursue this question. Prior research experience, projects, or relevant coursework should clearly build toward your proposed area of study, showing that you understand the tools and challenges involved. When your past work connects naturally to your research direction, it signals both preparation and intent.

If you do not yet have a well-defined question, a PhD may not be the right step right now.

Section 03 — Outcomes

What changes after an MS
vs staying in industry?

Roles, salary trajectories, and geography — a realistic look at what the MS credential actually buys you in the CS job market.

The honest answer is: it depends on what you want to do. In some specialisations, an MS is a significant accelerant. In others, two years of strong engineering experience will beat a degree. Here's the breakdown.

Roles and doors

MS advantage

Research engineering

At labs like DeepMind, Google Brain, Microsoft Research, Meta AI — a research-focused MS or PhD is essentially required. Most research engineer roles explicitly list it.

MS advantage

ML / AI specialisation

For senior ML roles (Staff ML Engineer and above), a graduate degree is common but not universal. Companies like Clearco, Swiggy, Flipkart hire strong self-taught engineers, but a degree accelerates the path.

Industry wins

Software engineering

For SWE roles, a BSc and strong coding skills are sufficient. Many top SWEs at FAANG/Indian startups do not have postgraduate degrees. The MS primarily shifts your starting level — L4 vs L3, for example.

Industry wins

Product, Design, PM

CS + work experience beats CS + MS for most product and design-adjacent roles. An MBA might eventually matter, but an MS rarely changes the trajectory here.

In the Indian market, the salary premium for an MS over a BS at the same company is meaningful but not dramatic — roughly one promotion's worth. For US roles, the premium is larger, especially in ML/AI.

Geography

If you want to work in India

MS is optional

A strong undergraduate record and good internship experience is sufficient for most roles. An IIT or IISc MS adds brand value but isn't a requirement. Indian startups and product companies hire on demonstrated ability.

If you want to work abroad

MS is often the visa pathway

For the US in particular, an MS on an F-1 visa with OPT (Optional Practical Training) is one of the most reliable routes to employment. The degree's ROI is partly the credential and partly the work authorisation it provides.

What an MS does not fix

An MS will not fix a weak understanding of fundamentals. It will make you a better engineer/researcher only if your fundamentals are already strong. Graduate programmes assume you are already competent; they are not remedial. Students should be entirely confident and comfortable with their undergraduate coursework and skillset before pursuing higher education.

An MS deepens your knowledge and opens specialised or research roles, while going straight to industry gives you immediate experience, income, and faster career momentum.

Section 05 — Opportunity Cost

Two years of MS
vs two years of work

What do you actually give up when you go to graduate school? The numbers, the compounding, and what most people forget to count.

Opportunity cost is what economists call the value of the next best alternative you give up. For most CS students from Ashoka, the question is: what does two years of a coursework MS cost you, compared to two years of working?

This is not a reason to avoid an MS. It is a reason to be clear about what you're trading.

The financial picture

Let's look at a rough comparison for an Ashoka CS graduate choosing between a US MS and direct employment.

US Coursework MS (2 years)

  • Tuition: $60,000–90,000 total
  • Living expenses: $30,000–40,000
  • Loan interest (if applicable)
  • Lost salary: $0 income while studying
  • TA/RA funding: rare in coursework MS
  • Total outflow: $75–110K before first paycheck

2 Years in Industry (India)

  • Starting salary: ₹10–18 LPA (good company)
  • Year 2 salary: ₹14–25 LPA with promotion
  • Skills: production systems, teamwork, shipping
  • Savings: meaningful capital accumulation
  • Network: industry peers, mentors
  • Total inflow: real money, real experience

The compounding effect

Money compounds. Skills compound. Reputation compounds. When you go to grad school at 22, you are not just delaying income for two years — you are starting the compounding clock two years later. At 30, two years earlier in your career can mean a meaningful difference in your savings, your seniority, and your network depth.

The same logic applies in reverse: if an MS unlocks a role that pays significantly more, that premium also compounds. The question is whether the specific MS you're considering actually does that for the roles you want.

What you can't buy back

Lost from working

Research training

The skills and habits of research — reading widely, thinking at the frontier, producing novel work — are hard to develop on the job. Industry provides breadth; research provides depth in a specific way.

Lost from studying

Early career momentum

The people you work with in your first two years become lifelong professional connections. Being two years behind your peers in seniority can matter in certain fast-moving fields like early-stage startups.

The PhD is different

A PhD in CS (especially in the US) is fully funded — you receive a stipend of $25,000–38,000/year and your tuition is waived. You are still giving up higher industry salaries, but you are not going into debt. The opportunity cost is real (especially in AI/ML, where industry salaries are very high) but the financial risk profile is much lower than a self-funded MS.

The PhD opportunity cost is primarily time — 4 to 6 years of your career spent at research-level pay rather than industry-level pay. In a field where compensation is very high, that gap can be $500K–$1M over the programme duration. This is not to say don't do a PhD. It is to say: know what you're choosing.

Questions to ask yourself

What specific role does this unlock?

Name a job title. Research whether companies hiring for it actually require or prefer the degree.

Can I get there without it in 3 years?

Strong engineers often reach senior roles within 3 years of graduating. Would 3 years of focused work get you to the same place as an MS?

What is my actual financial plan?

Loans taken at 22 shape decisions at 28. Model the numbers honestly, including interest and delayed savings.

Section 06 — Honest Advice

Who should not
do a PhD

This page doesn't appear on university brochures. It should. Most of the unhappiest PhD students saw these signs before they applied and didn't take them seriously.

A PhD is one of the best things you can do if you are the right person for it. It is one of the worst if you're not. The hard part is that many of the wrong reasons feel like the right reasons when you're 21.

Warning signs

⚠

You're doing it for the title

If your primary motivation is being called "Doctor" or the perceived prestige of a PhD, you will struggle. The daily experience of a PhD — failed experiments, rejected papers, unclear progress — is not compatible with prestige as a motivator. The title comes at the end of 5 years of often frustrating, unglamorous work.

⚠

You haven't actually tried research

Many students apply to PhD programmes based on having done well in courses, without having done independent research. Course performance and research performance are correlated but not the same thing. Before applying, spend time — genuinely — trying to read papers, identify gaps, and produce something original. If you find it boring or painful, that is important information.

⚠

You hate uncertainty

A PhD is structurally uncertain. You might work on something for a year and discover it doesn't work. Papers get rejected. Research directions change. Advisors leave. If you need clear milestones, predictable timelines, and regular external validation to function, a PhD will be very hard for you. Industry has reviews, sprints, and promotions. Research has rejection letters and silence.

⚠

You're avoiding a decision

Graduate school is sometimes chosen because it feels safer than the uncertainty of job hunting. This is especially true after a difficult graduation market. Pursuing a PhD because you're uncertain about what else to do is a recipe for misery — you will be stuck doing something very demanding without the internal drive that makes it worthwhile.

⚠

You want a high salary quickly

A PhD delays high earnings by 4–6 years. In CS, the industry salary curve is steep and starts early. If financial stability and early wealth accumulation are important to you right now, a PhD is not the fastest path. This is not a moral failing — it's just not aligned with that goal.

⚠

You haven't read papers and found them interesting

If you have never read a research paper and thought "I want to work on this problem," you are probably not ready for a PhD. Research motivation has to come from engagement with the actual content of a field — not from a vague interest in "AI" or "systems." If you can name a specific open problem that genuinely interests you, that's a good sign. If you can't, keep looking before committing.

Signs you might be well-suited

✓

You've done research and want more of it

Not a course project — independent research, reading papers, producing something. If you've had this experience and found it genuinely engaging (not just impressive-looking on a resume), that's a meaningful signal.

✓

There's a specific problem you want to solve

The best PhD students often have something they can't stop thinking about — a problem they find important and unsolved. It doesn't have to be a grand civilisational challenge. It has to be something you'd read about on a Sunday afternoon by choice.

✓

You're comfortable with delayed, uncertain feedback

You can work on something for months without knowing if it's working. You can rebound from rejection. You can motivate yourself without a deadline. These are rare skills and they matter enormously.

✓

You want a career path that requires it

Academic faculty positions require a PhD. Research scientist roles at frontier AI labs increasingly require one. If your specific goal has a PhD as a genuine prerequisite, that changes the calculus significantly.

"The students who thrive in PhD programmes are not necessarily the smartest. They are the most curious and the most resilient."

Section 07 — Action

What to test next

Before you commit to any path, there are low-cost experiments you can run. These take weeks or months — not years — and give you real signal on whether a direction is right for you.

The most important thing you can do before applying to a graduate programme is gather firsthand evidence about your own preferences. The actions below are designed to do that — not to pad a CV, but to genuinely test a hypothesis about yourself.

For students considering a PhD / Research MS

PhD test

Read 5 papers in an area that interests you

Not a survey — recent research papers (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, CVPR, SIGCOMM, depending on your area). Try to understand the problem, the proposed solution, and what's still open. Did you find this engaging or draining? That's your signal.

TIME: 2–3 weeks
PhD test

Email a faculty member at Ashoka or another institution

Ask if you can discuss their research area. A genuine interest in a researcher's work, communicated clearly, almost always gets a response. The conversation itself will tell you a lot about whether you find the field interesting when it's real and unpacked.

TIME: 1–4 weeks (including response time)
PhD test

Try to reproduce a paper's results

Pick a recent paper with open-source code. Try to run it, understand the architecture, and replicate a key result. This is as close to PhD-level daily work as you can get without enrolling. If you find it absorbing, that's real evidence. If you find it frustrating and pointless, that is also real evidence.

TIME: 1–2 months
Research MS test

Do an RA position with a faculty member

Ashoka has researchers whose work spans NLP, HCI, computational biology, and systems. Approach them for a research assistant role, even informally. Being inside a research project — cleaning data, running baselines, reading relevant papers — shows you what the work actually demands.

TIME: One semester

For students considering a coursework MS

MS test

Audit a graduate-level course online

Stanford CS229 (ML), MIT 6.824 (Distributed Systems), CMU 15-445 (Databases) are publicly available and genuinely graduate-level. Spend 4–6 weeks seriously engaging with one. If you find the pace and depth energising, that's a sign a coursework MS environment will suit you.

TIME: 4–8 weeks
MS test

Talk to someone one year out of a programme you're considering

Not someone on LinkedIn with a polished post. Someone who will tell you what was hard, what they wish they'd known, and whether they'd do it again. Ask specifically: what did the MS unlock that you couldn't have gotten otherwise?

TIME: One conversation

For students considering going directly to work

Industry test

Apply for and do a technical internship

If you haven't done a proper software engineering internship at a company with engineering culture (not a startup where you do everything), this should be your first move. It tells you whether you enjoy shipping production code, working in teams, and building at scale.

TIME: 2–3 months
Industry test

Build something real and get it used

Build a side project, deploy it, and try to get 100 real users. The discipline required — scoping, iterating, maintaining — is different from coursework. If you enjoy the feedback loop of building and shipping, industry likely suits you. If you find yourself more interested in the underlying algorithm than the product, that's a signal too.

TIME: 1–3 months

One final piece of advice

"Talk to people who changed their minds — the PhD student who left after two years, the engineer who went back to do a master's at 27. Their reasoning is more useful than the success stories."

The most honest information about any of these paths comes from people who took them and reconsidered. Seek out those conversations. They are rarely on a university's website or an alumni panel.
